THE SOGONG Hanok

How to stay in a hanok

A Korean hanok is a house of wood, paper and warm floors. It asks a little more of its guests — and gives back a lot.

Written by people who clean and look after guest stays in Seoul, learning hanok one house at a time. No booking, no ads — just what helps you and the house.

Chapter 1

Arriving — where do shoes go?

The floor inside a hanok is where people sit and sleep. Keeping it clean starts at the door.

  • Take your shoes off on the stone step before the wooden floor, and step up in socks or bare feet.
  • Leave shoes facing outward, side by side — easy to slip on when you leave.
  • Try not to step on the raised door sill; it is an old courtesy, and the wood wears there first.
  • Every house is a little different. If you are unsure, ask your host.
Chapter 2

The floor is your bed — ondol

Ondol means warm floors. The heat comes from below, so some spots are warmer than others.

  • Your bedding (yo and blanket) is laid on the floor at night and folded in the morning.
  • Do not leave phones, laptops or chocolate directly on a heated floor.
  • If it feels too warm or too cold, ask your host how to adjust the heating rather than guessing.
  • Folding your bedding in a corner before you leave is a small thing the next guest will feel.
Chapter 3

The door is paper — sound and light

Hanji paper lets in soft light, and also lets sound travel. Walls are thinner than you expect.

  • Keep voices low at night — your neighbors, and the room next door, can hear.
  • Open and close sliding doors by the wooden frame, not by pressing the paper.
  • Locks can be simple. Keep valuables in your bag.
  • Morning light comes early through the paper. An eye mask helps.
Chapter 4

Walking on wood — floors

The marks we wipe most often after a stay are suitcase-wheel lines, water rings from wet towels or cups, and scrapes from dragged chairs. Wood remembers all of them.

  • Carry your suitcase across wooden floors instead of rolling it.
  • Hang wet towels; don't leave them on the floor or on wooden tables.
  • Put cups on a tray or coaster, and wipe spills right away.
  • Lift chairs or low tables to move them — don't drag.
Chapter 5

Seasons — winter drafts, summer bugs

A hanok breathes with the seasons. That is part of its charm, and part of what to pack for.

  • Winter: expect some draft near doors and windows. Bring socks and a warm layer for the evening.
  • If the floor gets too hot, lay the bedding two layers thick or open a window a little — or ask your host.
  • The yo (floor mattress) can feel firmer than a bed. That is normal.
  • Summer: a wooden house lets in a few insects. Keep screens closed and don't leave doors open for long.
Chapter 6

Water, fire and food

A house made of wood and paper is careful about fire above everything else.

  • No smoking anywhere — inside, on the veranda, or in the courtyard.
  • No candles or open flames unless your host says so.
  • Many hanok stays don't allow cooking food with strong smoke or smell, like grilled meat or fish. Check before you cook.
  • Some hanok share a bathroom between rooms. If that matters to you, check before booking.
Chapter 7

The alley is home too — neighbors

Hanok neighborhoods are places where people live. The alley outside your gate is someone's front door.

  • Keep voices low in the courtyard and alley at night. Some areas, like Bukchon, have visitor hours for tourists.
  • Don't photograph other people's gates, yards or windows without asking.
  • Korea sorts trash strictly — general, recycling and food waste. If the signs are confusing, just keep it all together in one place and your host will sort it.
Chapter 8

Leaving — for the next guest

From the people who clean after you: three small things make the biggest difference.

  • Put used towels together in one place.
  • Bag your trash and leave it in one spot.
  • Empty the fridge and drawers of food you brought.
  • And if you can: fold the bedding in a corner, close the windows, and turn the heating as your host asked.
