How to stay in a hanok

A Korean hanok is a house of wood, paper and warm floors. It asks a little more of its guests — and gives back a lot.

Written by people who clean and look after guest stays in Seoul, learning hanok one house at a time. No booking, no ads — just what helps you and the house.

Chapter 1

Arriving — where do shoes go?

The floor inside a hanok is where people sit and sleep. Keeping it clean starts at the door.

  • Take your shoes off on the stone step before the wooden floor, and step up in socks or bare feet.
  • Leave shoes facing outward, side by side — easy to slip on when you leave.
  • Try not to step on the raised door sill; it is an old courtesy, and the wood wears there first.
  • Every house is a little different. If you are unsure, ask your host.
Chapter 2

The floor is your bed — ondol

Ondol means warm floors. The heat comes from below, so some spots are warmer than others.

  • Your bedding (yo and blanket) is laid on the floor at night and folded in the morning.
  • Do not leave phones, laptops or chocolate directly on a heated floor.
  • If it feels too warm or too cold, ask your host how to adjust the heating rather than guessing.
  • Folding your bedding in a corner before you leave is a small thing the next guest will feel.
Chapter 3

The door is paper — sound and light

Hanji paper lets in soft light, and also lets sound travel. Walls are thinner than you expect.

  • Keep voices low at night — your neighbors, and the room next door, can hear.
  • Open and close sliding doors by the wooden frame, not by pressing the paper.
  • Locks can be simple. Keep valuables in your bag.
  • Morning light comes early through the paper. An eye mask helps.
